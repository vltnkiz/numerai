Lesson 7 of Feature Neutralization

Why Everything Happens Per-Era

Every computation across Lessons 1–6 — correlation, exposure, neutralization — has quietly had an era attached to it. This lesson makes that explicit: why CORR is measured within each era rather than pooled across all rows, and why neutralization is applied the same way.

Primary source: numerai/example-scripts — feature_neutralization.ipynb, the official walkthrough notebook (you have a local copy in example-scripts/numerai/). Every code snippet below is quoted from it.

An era is Numerai's unit of "comparable"

An era is a snapshot: every stock in it was scored using the same time window, the same market conditions. Stocks within one era are directly comparable — you can meaningfully ask "did stock A outperform stock B this period?" Stocks in different eras are not directly comparable that way, because the market regime underneath them can be completely different. That's non-stationarity, the idea from Lesson 1 that feature-target relationships drift or reverse over time.

This one fact — comparability lives inside an era, not across eras — is the reason both CORR and neutralization get looped over eras instead of computed once on the whole dataset.

Why CORR is computed per-era

Here's the actual code, computing each feature's correlation with the target:

per_era_corr = train.groupby("era").apply( lambda d: numerai_corr(d[med_serenity_feats], d["target"]) )

groupby("era") splits the data into one cross-section per era, and numerai_corr runs separately on each slice. Two reasons this matters, not one:

1. It's literally how you get paid. Numerai's tournament doesn't score you with one correlation computed over every row you've ever submitted. It computes a correlation per era, then averages those per-era numbers. A practice metric that pools all rows together is measuring something your payout doesn't depend on — so if you want a number that predicts your score, it has to be built the same way the score is built.

2. Pooling would let one era dominate, and reward the wrong thing. Imagine one era happens to have unusually large target spreads, or a feature with an unusually wide range that period. A single correlation computed across all rows at once lets that one era's scale swamp the signal from every other era — you'd be measuring "did this feature line up with the target in the biggest era," not "does this feature reliably help, era after era." Averaging per-era correlations instead treats every era as one vote, regardless of how extreme its values happened to be.

Why neutralization is also looped per-era

Same pattern, this time for neutralizing predictions against features:

neutralized = validation.groupby("era", group_keys=True).apply( lambda d: neutralize( d[["prediction"]], d[med_serenity_feats], proportion=proportion ) )

Recall from Lesson 5: neutralization solves β = pinv(X) · prediction, where X is the feature matrix, then subtracts proportion × Xβ from the predictions. The question is: what rows should be stacked into X when solving for β?

If you stacked every era into one giant X, pinv(X) would solve for a single β per feature that best fits the fitted-vs-prediction relationship averaged over the entire non-stationary history. But you already know from Lesson 1 that a feature's relationship to the target (and therefore to predictions built from it) can reverse between eras. A β that's a compromise across regimes that disagree with each other fits none of them well — it's the same failure mode as pooling CORR, just one step further downstream.

Looping per era instead solves a fresh β for each era's own cross-section of stocks. The neutralization removes exactly the linear relationship between predictions and features as they actually stood in that snapshot — not some blended average that may not describe any single era. This keeps neutralization doing its actual job: stripping out the part of a prediction explainable by feature values, measured honestly against the conditions the model will be scored under, one era at a time.

A feature has β ≈ +0.4 (positive relationship to predictions) in the first half of training eras, and β ≈ −0.4 (negative relationship) in the second half. What happens if you neutralize using one β solved across all eras pooled together, versus one β solved per era?

The same idea, twice

Both halves of this lesson are one idea wearing two hats: an era is the unit within which stocks are comparable, because it's the unit within which market conditions — and therefore feature-target relationships — are held roughly fixed. CORR measures how well predictions line up with the target inside that unit, then averages across units to avoid letting any one era's scale or one part of history's regime dominate. Neutralization removes the feature-explained part of a prediction inside that same unit, for the identical reason: a β averaged across regimes that disagree describes neither of them.

If it's not yet clear why the tournament's actual payout formula (rather than just "more correlation is better") is itself computed as an average of per-era numbers, ask your teaching agent — it's the same non-stationarity argument from Lesson 1, one level up.
