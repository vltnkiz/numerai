Lesson 2 of Feature Neutralization

Correlation & Spearman Rank Correlation

The one piece of math that Lesson 1 leaned on without explaining — worth nailing down before Lesson 3, where we build feature neutralization on top of it.

Primary source: the exposure formula this refreshes comes straight from "Model Diagnostics: Feature Exposure" — this lesson just unpacks the statistics it assumes.

Correlation: "do two things move together?"

Correlation is a single number between −1 and 1 that summarizes how two variables move relative to each other.

  • +1: perfectly move together (one goes up, the other always goes up too)
  • 0: no consistent relationship
  • −1: perfectly move opposite (one goes up, the other always goes down)

The standard version — Pearson correlation — is built from two ideas you may already have intuitions for: covariance (do they move together, in raw units?) and standard deviation (how much does each one move on its own?). Dividing the first by the second two rescales the answer so it always lands between −1 and 1, regardless of the units either variable is measured in.

r = covariance(X, Y) / (std_dev(X) * std_dev(Y))

You won't need to compute this by hand from raw covariance — every stats/ML library has a one-line call for it. What matters is the intuition: correlation asks "as X goes up, does Y tend to go up (or down) proportionally?"

The catch: Pearson only sees straight lines

Pearson correlation measures linear relationships. If Y always increases when X increases, but not at a constant rate — say Y grows like X² — Pearson correlation will report something less than 1, even though the relationship is perfectly predictable. Pearson is blind to relationships that curve.

A model's predictions and a feature rarely have a clean straight-line relationship. What we actually care about for feature exposure is weaker but more general: does a higher feature value tend to go with a higher prediction, regardless of the exact shape of that trend? That's a monotonic relationship — consistently increasing (or decreasing), even if not at a constant rate. This is exactly what Spearman correlation measures.

Spearman: correlation on ranks, not values

Spearman rank correlation has a strikingly simple definition: replace every value with its rank (1st smallest, 2nd smallest, …), then compute ordinary Pearson correlation on those ranks instead of the raw values.

Because ranks only encode order, not magnitude, Spearman correlation no longer cares whether the relationship is a straight line, a curve, or any other shape — only whether it's consistently increasing or decreasing. That's exactly the property Numerai's feature exposure calculation wants: does the model's prediction rank move consistently with a feature's rank, however that trend bends?

Worked example

Five rows. Prediction and one feature value per row:

row: A B C D E prediction: 0.10 0.90 0.30 0.70 0.50 feature: 1 50 2 40 3

Convert each column to ranks (1 = smallest):

row: A B C D E prediction rank: 1 5 2 4 3 feature rank: 1 5 2 4 3

The ranks are identical between the two columns — every time prediction rank goes up, feature rank goes up by exactly the same amount. Spearman correlation here is 1.0, a perfect monotonic relationship — even though the raw feature values (1, 50, 2, 40, 3) don't increase at anything like a constant rate relative to the predictions (0.10, 0.90, 0.30, 0.70, 0.50). Pearson on the raw values would report something high but not exactly 1; Spearman, working on ranks alone, reports exactly 1, because rank order is all it looks at.

Why does Numerai's feature exposure calculation use Spearman correlation rather than Pearson correlation?
Ranks for prediction: [1, 2, 3, 4]. Ranks for a feature, same rows: [4, 3, 2, 1] (perfectly reversed order). What is the Spearman rank correlation? (one number, e.g. 0.50)

With that in hand, "feature exposure" from Lesson 1 should now read as a fully concrete recipe: rank the predictions, rank each feature, correlate the two rank lists. Lesson 3 builds on this same idea — but instead of just measuring reliance on a feature, it removes it.

If covariance, standard deviation, or the ranking step still feels shaky, ask your teaching agent to walk through another worked example — better to ask now than to carry a shaky foundation into neutralization's regression math.
