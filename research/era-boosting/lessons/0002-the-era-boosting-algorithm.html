Lesson 2 of Era Boosting

The Era Boosting Algorithm

Lesson 1 established the goal: stop optimizing for mean correlation alone, and optimize for stationarity too. Era boosting is richai's answer — a training-time change with no new math, just a change in which rows get to teach the model.

Primary source: read "Era Boosted Models" by richai on the Numerai forum before or after this lesson — everything here is grounded in it.

The idea in one sentence

Ordinary gradient boosting adds each new tree to reduce error everywhere, weighted by how much data sits where. Era boosting adds each new tree to reduce error only in the eras that are currently losing — so the model can't keep ignoring its worst eras in favor of eras it already handles well.

The loop

Four steps, repeated num_iters times:

1. Build trees_per_step new trees on the training data currently in play. 2. Score every era in the full training set separately (per-era Spearman correlation). 3. Rank eras worst → best. Keep only the worst proportion of them (e.g. bottom 50%). 4. Set "data currently in play" = those worst eras. Go to 1.

Round after round, the pool of eras a new tree is allowed to learn from shrinks down to whichever eras the model is currently failing hardest. Each new batch of trees is a targeted correction, not another vote for the eras that were already fine.

Walking it through on 6 eras

Suppose proportion=0.5, trees_per_step=1, and after building tree 1 on all 6 eras you score per-era correlation:

Era: 1 2 3 4 5 6 Corr: 0.03 -0.01 0.05 -0.04 0.02 0.06

Ranked worst to best: 4 (−0.04), 2 (−0.01), 5 (0.02), 1 (0.03), 3 (0.05), 6 (0.06). With proportion=0.5, keep the bottom half: eras 4, 2, 5. Tree 2 is built using only rows from eras 4, 2, and 5 — eras 1, 3, and 6 sit this round out entirely, because the model doesn't need help there yet.

Next round, all 6 eras get re-scored using the model as it now stands (tree 1 + tree 2 combined), and the worst half is selected again — not necessarily the same three eras. An era that was struggling can graduate out of the "worst half" once the targeted trees fix it, and a previously-fine era can drop in if it's now comparatively weak.

Why this pushes toward stationarity, not just mean correlation

A model trained the ordinary way has no mechanism that notices "these eras keep losing" — it just minimizes overall error, and a handful of chronically bad eras barely move that average. Era boosting makes chronic underperformance visible and directly targetable: an era that's always in the bottom half keeps getting extra dedicated trees, round after round, until it either improves or the model runs out of rounds. That's the direct link back to lesson 1 — this is a training procedure aimed at reducing the clumpy, autocorrelated losses that plain mean-correlation training doesn't "see."

The reported result — and its caveat

richai's post reports in-sample Sharpe rising from 2.28 to 21.99 over 200 trees (trees_per_step=10, num_iters=20) using era boosting versus ordinary training. That's a large number, and it comes with an explicit, important caveat stated in the original post: this was in-sample — out-of-sample validation had not been done yet at the time of writing. A model can look dramatically more stationary on the exact data it was fit to without that gain surviving on new eras. Keep this caveat attached to the headline number whenever you cite it.

What era boosting is not

It's a training-time technique — it changes what data each tree sees while the model is being built. That's a different lever from feature neutralization (a post-processing technique applied to an already-trained model's predictions, covered in the sibling workspace). The two aren't competitors; they target overlapping problems from different points in the pipeline, and a model can use both.
