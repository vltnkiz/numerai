Lesson 1 of Era Boosting

What Is Performance Stationarity?

Two models can share the same average return and still carry very different risk. Stationarity is the property that tells them apart.

Primary source: read "Performance Stationarity" by richai on the Numerai forum before or after this lesson — everything here is grounded in it.

Same average, different risk

Same average. Very different experience of holding the model. Model B has two brutal stretches — five losses in a row, then five more — before it ever pays off. Model A never strings together more than one loss at a time. If you're staking real money on Numerai, A is the model you can sleep at night with. B is the one that gets you to sell at the bottom of a drawdown, right before it turns around.

The concept: stationarity as "no memory"

richai's framing: a model's sequence of good and bad eras should behave like flipping a biased coin — "lacking autocorrelation / being memoryless / having no long burn periods." Autocorrelation here just means: does knowing today's result help you predict tomorrow's? For a fair (or biased-but-independent) coin, no — every flip is its own event. Model A's sequence looks like that. Model B's doesn't: once it's in a bad patch, the bad patch tends to continue. That's positive autocorrelation in the losses, and it's exactly what "non-stationary performance" means in this context.

This is a specific, narrower use of "stationary" than the general statistics term (which is about a process's statistical properties — mean, variance — not changing over time). Here, it's applied specifically to the sequence of era-by-era performance: does the win/loss pattern look memoryless, or does it look clumpy?

Why this happens: the features aren't stationary either

richai traces the cause upstream: stock-market features like value and momentum are themselves non-stationary — they cycle through regimes where they work well, then regimes where they don't, and back again. A model trained on these features, without any special care, inherits that instability. It performs well while its favored features are "in regime," and drops hard when they aren't. This is the same underlying idea as feature exposure from the sibling neutralization lessons — reliance on unstable features is the mechanism, and reducing that reliance (via neutralization, or as you'll see next, via a training-time fix) is how you address it.

Why mean return isn't enough

The Sharpe ratio — mean return divided by standard deviation of returns — already penalizes variance. But it treats a scattered set of bad eras the same as five bad eras in a row, as long as the total variance matches. richai's post introduces Smart Sharpe to close that gap: a Sharpe ratio with an extra penalty for autocorrelation.

smart_sharpe(x) = mean(x) / (std(x) × autocorr_penalty(x))

You don't need the internals of autocorr_penalty yet — the point to take away is just what it's for: two models can have identical plain Sharpe ratios and different Smart Sharpe ratios, because Smart Sharpe additionally punishes the "clumpy" model. It's a way of putting a number on the Model A vs. Model B distinction above.

The open question

richai poses this as a question, not a solved problem: should models be trained to directly optimize for stationarity, rather than just mean correlation? That question is exactly what the next lesson's technique — era boosting — is an attempt to answer.
