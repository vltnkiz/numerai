Lesson 1 of Feature Neutralization

What Is Feature Exposure?

Why a model that scores well in backtests can still blow up on Numerai — and how to measure the risk before it happens.

Primary source: read "Model Diagnostics: Feature Exposure" on the Numerai forum before or after this lesson — everything here is grounded in it.

The problem: features aren't stable

Numerai's data is organized into eras — roughly, time periods. A feature that strongly predicts the target in one era can be useless, or even actively misleading, in another. This is called non-stationarity: the statistical relationship between feature and target shifts over time, because markets change regime.

Now imagine a model that has learned to lean heavily on just one or two features — say, because those features happened to work great across most of the training eras. As long as the market keeps behaving the way it did in training, the model looks great. The moment the regime shifts and those particular features stop working, the model's predictions collapse all at once. One Numerai participant described this happening to their own model: it performed well for a long stretch, then "burned" hard when a regime change hit the features it had over-relied on.

The insight: measure reliance, not just accuracy

A model's overall correlation with the target tells you how good its predictions are on average. It tells you nothing about how it got there — whether that skill is spread across many features (robust) or concentrated in a few (fragile). Feature exposure is the diagnostic that measures the second thing.

Formally: for each feature, take the Spearman rank correlation between the model's predictions and that feature's values, across all rows. Spearman correlation is just Pearson correlation computed on the ranks of the values rather than the raw values — it asks "do high-ranked predictions tend to come with high-ranked feature values?" without caring about the exact shape of the relationship.

Do this for every feature and you get one number per feature: that feature's exposure. A feature the model barely uses will have an exposure near 0. A feature the model leans on heavily — directly or through strong correlation with what it does use — will have an exposure with large absolute value (close to 1 or −1).

# exposure of one feature = Spearman correlation(predictions, feature) feature_exposures = [spearman_corr(predictions, df[f]) for f in feature_columns] max_feature_exposure = max(abs(e) for e in feature_exposures)

The forum post reports a validation max_feature_exposure of 0.2905 for a reasonably good model — treated as acceptable but improvable. There's also an aggregate version, the root-mean-square across all features, which captures overall reliance rather than just the single worst offender:

rms_feature_exposure = sqrt(mean(e**2 for e in feature_exposures))

The evidence

This isn't just a theoretical concern. Testing across 80+ models (gradient-boosted trees and neural nets), the author found a consistent inverse relationship: models with lower in-sample max feature exposure reliably had better out-of-sample performance. Low exposure, measured before you ever see new data, predicted robustness to regime change.

The catch

You can't just chase exposure to zero. The post is explicit about this tension: models with very low max feature exposure also tend to have low correlation with the target. Some feature reliance is exactly where the predictive signal lives — a model with zero exposure to everything has learned nothing. The goal isn't "minimize exposure," it's "get the best consistency-for-correlation tradeoff." (That tradeoff — and a specific technique for improving it, feature neutralization — is the subject of the next lesson.)
