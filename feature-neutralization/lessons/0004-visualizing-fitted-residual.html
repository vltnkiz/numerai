Lesson 4 of Feature Neutralization

Seeing the Split

Lesson 3 gave you the algebra: prediction = fitted + residual, and neutralized = prediction − proportion × fitted. This lesson turns that into a picture you can drag, using the same five rows.

Primary source: "An Introduction to Feature Neutralization & Exposure" — this lesson visualizes the same least-squares procedure that post walks through numerically.

The five rows, plotted

Each point is one row: feature on the x-axis, prediction on the y-axis. The line is the best-fit line from Lesson 3 — the one that minimizes total squared distance to the points. For each point, the fitted value is where it lands on the line; the residual is the gap between the point and the line.

Drag the slider. At proportion = 0 the orange points sit exactly on top of the blue ones — nothing has been removed. As proportion rises, each orange point slides from its original position toward its residual — straight down toward (or up from) the fitted line. By proportion = 1, every orange point is its residual, and the correlation readout should sit near zero: the systematic left-to-right climb is gone, leaving a flat, uncorrelated scatter.

Why removing the fitted part actually kills the exposure

This isn't a coincidence of this particular data — it's a guarantee of how least squares works. The fitted line is defined as the one that minimizes squared residual distance, and the point that minimizes that distance is always the one where the leftover gap is perpendicular to the feature direction. A residual that's perpendicular to the feature has, by construction, zero correlation with it. So:

  • fitted = the entire part of the prediction that a straight line through the feature can explain — this is exactly what "feature exposure" (Lessons 1–2) is measuring
  • residual = everything left over, which is mathematically guaranteed to have zero (Pearson) correlation with the feature

Subtracting the fitted part isn't a heuristic that happens to reduce exposure — it's the one quantity whose removal drives that specific relationship to exactly zero, while leaving everything else in the prediction untouched.

One honest caveat: exposure is measured with Spearman (rank) correlation, but the regression here is ordinary least squares, which targets Pearson correlation. Removing the OLS-fitted part drives Pearson correlation to exactly zero and, in practice, drags the rank-based Spearman exposure down with it too — but the two aren't algebraically identical. That's why real diagnostics still re-check Spearman exposure on the neutralized predictions afterward rather than assuming zero.
